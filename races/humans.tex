\noindent
Humans are a massive, war-like race with dwarven ears, who live in artificial
caves they build from wood.  Their strong arms let them wield long, iron
weapons for battle, and build high wooden walls and dig deep ditches around
their \glspl{village}.  Their large size and tall walls don't protect all of
the humans from being eaten by large creatures of the forest, so populations
must survive by having many children.  Humans reproduce at an alarming rate ---
each pair can produce up to one child per year.

A subtle chaos hides within their \glspl{village}.  No matter how long one
lives with them, each one does the same thing, at the same time of day, every
day.  But when people return a dozen years later, they find half the humans
have a different routine, and the other half have died.

When enough humans form together, a town often springs up in the centre,
organically.  Once a town establishes itself in the area, humans will start to
raise specialists who can practice at woodworking, book-binding, and other
specialized skills.  The town and specialists form slowly, by a mysterious
human instinct, and without any community planning.

\subsubsection[The Human Language]{The Language}
\index{Languages!Humans}
looks very unified in speech, but with different styles of writing.
This illusion comes from the fact that every time humans travel, they pick up a few local words, and copy a little of the local accent.
By the time someone has travelled a thousand miles, they have arrived at a very different language, without learning anything entirely new at any step.

Exactly what counts as a new language depends a little on shared words, and a lot on the speed of travel.
Simply put:

$$ comprehension = \frac{cromulance}{velocity} $$

The local human languages share enough words with Gnomish that the two are mutually intelligible, as long as both speakers have some patience.
However, when humans speak their local dialects quickly, nobody can understand them except their relatives and others who come from a nearby \gls{village}, or from the same town.
Many use their dialects as a secret language, or `cant', when they want to speak privately.

\subsubsection{The Structure}
of any \gls{village} or town is extremely important, because humans love
hierarchies and become confused about what they are doing if they cannot
identify a nearby leader.  As a result, specialized decision-makers arise,
usually inside cities, called `\glspl{warden}', who dictate what happens in a
town, resolve disputes, and decide who joins the \glspl{guard}.

\subsubsection{Humanity's Curse}
slowly destroys every settlement they build, bit by bit.  Each year the crops grow a little thinner, until farmers can't plough enough to trade, then don't have enough to eat.
Those who live in outer \glspl{village}, far from a town, often begin building a new \gls{village} after a few decades.
But the inner hamlets by the walls of a town must farm more land for a lower yeild, or simply leave the land barren until it recovers.

Most believe that human blood causes the slow degradation of soil, so the more people die in a settlement, the worse the crops become.

\subsection{Commerce}

Humans' massive feet and their habit of following each other has created a massive road.
Carrying goods in carts, and driving farmed animals down the same paths helps define and widen the roads.
Many humans believe that their road joins all humans together in a grand web, which stretches around all of \gls{fenestra}, between the inhospitable coast (which destroys all structures with giant waves at the end of each \gls{cycle}), and the great \gls{blight} in the centre of the forest.

Humans cannot weave quality \glspl{spell}, or make long-lasting armour, but the sheer quantity of goods they have to trade always lets them purchase these goods from others.

\subsection{Inheritance}

\subsubsection[Marching Legs: every \glsentrytext{ep} spent to march adds 2 miles]{Marching Legs}
\label{humanInheritance}
mean humans can walk a long way before they feel tired.
Their stamina comes from size, and the propensity to sweat more than any other creature (which is also why humans smell like that).

Instead of taking \pgls{ep} to cover an extra mile, they can take \pgls{ep} to cover an additional 2~miles.

Humans cannot sprint like wild animals, but when time is measured in days, they are the fastest creatures \gls{fenestra}.

\subsubsection{Disease Immunity}
is common among humans because they live in cramped communities with massive populations (hundreds of humans in one place is not even a large settlement).
These communities host dozens of diseases every year, blossoming according to the season like a garden of boils and coughs.

Human \glspl{pc} can spend \pgls{storypoint} to note their immunity to a disease, along with a story of how the disease hit their community earlier in life.

\subsubsection{Fate \Glsfmtplural{ingredient}}
\index[mana]{Fate!Humans}
can be made from human blood, if distilled into an ink.
Sometimes it makes for two \glspl{ingredient}, if the human had enough blood.

This process takes a long time, but the results speak for themselves, so many spell-casters will refuse to enter battle until they have enough humans to craft some bloody \glspl{boon}.

\subsection{Enlistment}

The \glspl{temple} come from humans, so the \gls{templeOfBeasts} has more humans than any other kind of people.
However, it also has more non-humans than any other \gls{temple}, since it remains eternally open, and tends to pick up stragglers, weirdos, and optimists from all walks of live.

\subsection{Roleplaying Humans}
\index{Humans!Roleplaying}

If something doesn't work, humans just try a different method.
If they can't buy what they want, they often just steal.
When they can't figure out \pgls{spell} to make the plants grow, they turn to studying natural cultivation.
And when they can't open a door, they start hammering the walls.

Humans may seem slow when working on a task, but come back a year later and you can often find them with the same goal, using a new technique.
