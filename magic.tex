\chapter{Witchcraft}
\label{witchcraft}

\noindent
\Glspl{spell}, \glspl{talisman}, and \gls{witchcraft} of every kind follow the same formula, called \pgls{invocation} --- a primitive sentence, never spoken but heavily implied.
The actual \gls{casting} could express the \gls{spell} as chatter, humming, sign-language, theatre or song; they all look different, but all express that same \gls{invocation}.
Once \pgls{witch} has sufficient practice with the basic \glspl{spell}, they can make their own during \gls{downtime}.

\section{Weaving Spells}
\label{spellWeaving}

These are the conceptual steps you can use to create \glspl{spell} during \gls{downtime}.
\iftoggle{core}{%
  Once you have \pgls{spell} on-paper, check the \textit{Core Rules}, \autopageref{make_spell}, to see if your character can manage the task, or what might happen if they fail.
}{}

\begin{multicols}{2}

\subsection{Elemental \Glsfmtplural{sphere}}

\noindent
Each \gls{sphere} has four \glspl{spell} at the first level --- one to Wax, another to Wane, one to Warp, and one to Witness.
This makes twenty Elemental \glspl{spell} in total.

\glsadd{Water}
\subsubsection[Water]{\gls{Water}~Water}
wants to flow downwards, clear and pure, and join with other water.
\Glspl{witch} can encourage crystal-clear rivers to flow faster easily, but small receptacles or muddy liquids resist \glspl{spell}, raising the \gls{tn}.

\begin{description}
  \item[Waxing Water]
  accelerates and distorts its flow. If the flow is circular or unevenly
  distributed, a vortex may form and cause the water to overflow the edges of
  its basin.  Targets caught in a vortex are pulled down, losing \glspl{ap} in
  combat.  Wax Water is resisted by sealed containers but bursts them
  explosively if successful.

  \begin{description}
    \item[Detailed Waxing]
    can also call it forth in liquid form from damp earth or air, though arid
    places make this more difficult.
  \end{description}

  \item[Waning Water]
  makes it slow sluggish, slow, and polluted.

  \begin{description}
    \item[Detailed Waning]
    \glspl{spell} remove water's purity, making it sticky, like a web.
  \end{description}

  \item[Warping Water]
  can scatter it to a fine mist (like Air) or hardens it to ice (like Earth).

  \item[Witnessing Water]
  tells \pgls{witch} where water runs.
  A clear stream has \tn[7], while detecting ale or a stagnant pond might have \tn[10].
  A measure of blood in a glass phial would push the \gls{tn} to 16 as it has little left of the innate form of water.
\end{description}

\glsadd{Fate}
\subsubsection[Fate]{\gls{Fate}~Fate}
is a sorting mechanism, making sense of the world.
It encourages the brave, confident, and beautiful, and leaves cowards to the whims of a meaningless world.
Fate governs \glsentrylongpl{fp} and \glspl{segment} (including random encounters).

\begin{description}
  \item[Waxing Fate]
  means gaining \glspl{fp}, which enhances one's sense of courage and certainty.

  \begin{description}
    \item[Detailed Wax Fate]
    \glspl{spell} don't just rejuvenate, but increase a target's maximum \glspl{fp}.
  \end{description}

  \item[Waning Fate]
  depletes \glspl{fp}, and afflicts people with a sense of dread and uncertainty.

  \begin{description}
    \item[Detailed Waning Fate]
    \glspl{spell} diminish a target's maximum \glspl{fp}.
  \end{description}

  \item[Warping Fate]
  can push someone to encounter the next \pgls{segment} faster than they otherwise would, or shift an encounter to the `wrong' person.
  Warping \glspl{spell} may also make someone's \glspl{fp} feel unfamiliar, so other \glspl{spell} cannot detect those \glspl{fp} so easily (but they still operate the same).

  \item[Witnessing Fate]
  lets someone detect \glspl{fp}.
  Casting these \glspl{spell} on a crowd can pinpoint particularly wilful individuals, or detect someone protected by Fate \glspl{spell} (since only magic can allow someone to gain large amounts of \glspl{fp}).
\end{description}

\glsadd{Air}
\subsubsection[Air]{\gls{Air}~Air}
is light and clear, swirling everywhere.
Degraded air becomes impure dust or stagnant snow.

\begin{description}
  \item[Waxing Air]
  Waxing Air gathers and compresses it while controlling the rate and direction
  of its release. This is easier in the presence of strong winds, because that
  energy can be redirected rather than induced. Wax Air is resisted by dusty,
  stale, or humid air.

  When producing a gust of wind to knock someone off balance, if the spell
  succeeds the roll is also compared to \roll{Strength}{Athletics} of any
  targets potentially affected.  Those which fall short during combat
  \glspl{round} lose \glspl{ap} equal to the \gls{spell}'s level + 2.

  \item[Waning Air]
  makes it rancid, inflicting \glspl{ep} on anyone breathing it in.
  These noxious Air \glspl{spell} can be easily avoided by holding one's breath%
  \exRef{core}{Core Rules}{holdingBreath} 
  and taking \pgls{step} beyond the smog.

  \item[Warping Air]
  can give it some semblance of substance, making it misty, and inflicting Penalties to vision or ranged attacks.
  Or the permeable nature may change to solid at the edge, forming a barrier of tension, and creating a bubble which allows passage underwater.

  \item[Witnessing Air]
  \glspl{spell} provide the only sane way to find underground tunnels.
  Making guesses about what ground might hold a cavity takes a long time, but it works better than digging random holes.
\end{description}

\glsadd{Fire}
\subsubsection[Fire]{\gls{Fire}~Fire}
is rooted in consumption and reaches jealously toward the stars.
It simplifies complex, static structures into dust.
It is change and renewal.

\begin{description}
  \item[Waxing Fire]
  makes it flare up, consume faster, and reach out.
  \begin{description}
    \item[Detailed Waxing Fire]
    \glspl{spell} can make a fire flare up in a particular direction, leaping at someone nearby.
  \end{description}

  \item[Waning Fire]
  Waning Fire diminishes it. Candles or \glspl{torch} extinguish, cooking fires
  are reduced to coals, and bonfires retreat fitfully from their edges.

  \item[Warping Fire]
  can change the colour of the flame, make it as cold as earth, or make it `spill' outwards like water (while still emerging from the fuel it feeds on).

  \item[Witnessing Fire]
  allows casters to detect Fire at the usual, short range.
  The truest test of \pgls{witch} is whether or not they can find a use for this \gls{spell}.
\end{description}

\glsadd{Earth}
\subsubsection[Earth]{\gls{Earth}~Earth}
wants to stay solid, uniform, and stagnant.
Targeting clay or ice has the standard \tn[7], but trying to affect complex materials like wood or multicoloured rocks raises the \gls{tn} to 12; and harder materials, like iron or bronze raises the \gls{tn} to 14 or more.
Steel --- a complex and very durable material --- raises the \gls{tn} to at least 16.

\begin{description}
  \item[Waxing Earth]
  makes it more contiguous, more solid, and finally more sharply pronounced.
  Mountains are peak Earth.  Sand binds to a single block, and snow turns to
  ice.

  \item[Waning Earth]
  first fractures it and breaks it into fragments, then
  rounds those fragments and grinds them into dust.

  \item[Warping Earth]
  can make material transparent, like glass, or make it slowly flow, like treacle.

  \item[Witnessing Earth]
  lets a caster detect solid mass.
  \begin{description}
    \item[Duplicated Witness Earth]
    \glspl{spell} let \glspl{witch} map out a space in the dark by asking about many points in space, while \textit{Detailed Witness} \glspl{spell} let them detect specific materials, like `gold' or `leather'.
  \end{description}
\end{description}

\needspace{8\baselineskip}
\subsection{High \Glsfmtplural{sphere}}

Using the High \glspl{sphere} doesn't require a new \gls{skill} -- casters simply combine two Elemental \glspl{sphere}.
Fire and Earth make Force; Earth and Water make Life.
The High \gls{sphere} rating equals the lowest of the two.

The spells listed across the \textit{Core Rules} don't list the High \glspl{sphere}, so players can simply look at any spell's requirements and note whether or not they have the right \glspl{skill}.

Check the diagram \vpageref{speltogram} for the various combinations.

\glsadd{Mind}
\subsubsection[Mind]{\gls{Mind}~Mind}
divides experiences into names, then uses the names to tell stories.
Mind \glspl{spell} affect Mind \glspl{attribute} and \glspl{restingaction}.
It can indirectly affect \glspl{fp} by altering someone's Charisma Bonus, or bloom thoughts into concrete plans.

People can resist the tricks these \glspl{spell} play by recognizing the manipulation quickly, and holding to steadfast, rigid, facts so people usually resist these \glspl{spell} with \roll{Wits}{Academics}.
Smaller minds, without much capacity for names can raise the \gls{tn}.
Smaller birds might be at \tn[9], while invertebrae usually have \pgls{tn} of 12 or more.

All \glspl{spell} have some small fragment of a Mind, so Mind \glspl{spell} can target other \glspl{spell},%
\footnote{The Mind \glsfmttext{spell} itself becomes one with the \glsfmttext{spell} it affects, so meta-mind \glsfmtplural{spell} are simply another Mind \glsfmttext{spell}, and cannot create regression, paradoxes, or Philosophical problems.}
allowing them to make decisions, and co\"ordinate with other \glspl{spell}.

%\bigbreak

\begin{description}
  \item[Waxing Mind]
  blooms an idea into obsession.
  Enhancing a dream can stop someone waking up, and enhancing their will to study can focus them on studying to the detriment of everything else.
  The surest sign of a useless \gls{witch} is over-reliance on study-\glspl{spell}, as they can stop the \gls{witch} doing anything else until the task has been completed.

  \begin{description}
    \item[Detailed Wax Mind]
    \glspl{spell} can imbue the primitive, single, thought which created \pgls{spell} with an Intelligence Bonus.
  \end{description}

  \item[Waning Mind]
  dissolves names until someone cannot think clearly about a topic.
  Memories and plans become cloudy, and motivation dies.

  \item[Warping Mind]
  can show people new concepts by recombining the ideas they already have.

  \begin{description}
    \item[Detailed Warp]
    \glspl{spell} can make \glspl{spell} \emph{sociable}, giving them some basic pack-instincts.
  \end{description}

  \item[Witnessing Mind]
  \glspl{spell} detect thoughts.
\end{description}

\glsadd{Death}
\subsubsection[Death]{\gls{Death}~Death}
removes rubbish, and recycles the broken.
It affects animals and plants, including wood.

  Healthy people, without a mark of bad health, or a single weary joint, remain completely unaffected by Death \glspl{spell}.
  However, those people are mostly a myth --- even a single \glsentrylong{ep} shows enough weakness for Death \glspl{spell} to exploit.

\begin{description}
  \item[Waxing Death]
  opens scars, makes illness worse, and generally increase \glspl{ep}.

  Death \glspl{spell} struggle to target large, healthy creatures.
  Targets can resist Waxing \glspl{spell} with their current \glsentrylongpl{hp}.

  \item[Detailed Wax] \glspl{spell} can remove \glspl{hp} \emph{directly} instead of \glspl{ep}, without regard for \glspl{fp} or \gls{dr}.

  \item[Waning Death]
  arrests or immunises a type of degradation.
  Damage and \glsentrylongpl{ep} raise the \glsentrylong{tn}.

  \item[Detailed Waning]
  \glspl{spell} can make the target more like \pgls{undead} creature, and gain some limited immunity from all manner of \glspl{ep}~Penalties.

  \item[Warping Death]
  lets people access the benefits of death without all the baggage.
  They can see as the dead see, speak with the undead and \glspl{artefact}, and slow their bodily functions so as not to accrue \glsentrylongpl{ep}.

  \item[Witnessing Death]
  detects corpses or those soon-to-be corpses.
  The not-yet dead add their current \glspl{hp}.
\end{description}

\glsadd{Light}
\subsubsection[Light]{\gls{Light}~Light}
also covers `sound' --- it covers the sensory effects which emanate.

\begin{description}
  \item[Waxing Light]
  means making any light or sound bloom, making a voice echo and resonate, or pushing a candle's light into every crevice in the room.
  These \glspl{spell} can momentarily blind people accustomed to the darkness, but have very little effect when cast in daylight.

  Light \glspl{spell} target the light itself, so when a single candle blooms, it can affect an entire room.

  \begin{description}
    \item[Detailed, Waxing Light]
    can duplicate an appearance, producing mirror-images or echoes of a target.
  \end{description}

  \item[Waning Light]
  destroys light and creates shadows; strong light can destroy or negate these \glspl{spell}, and direct Sunlight pushes the \gls{tn} up to at least 12.

  \item[Warping Light]
  swaps colours, makes deep voices high-pitched, or just makes a pig's
  breakfast out of what something seems like.  This makes for an excellent
  make-shift mask, as few can tell what's under the distorted mess of sound and
  shifting hues.

  People can try to figure out who's really there with \roll{Wits}{Empathy}.

  \begin{description}
    \item[Detailed Warping Light]
    creates illusions.
    These work as well in darkness or light (as they have more substance to work with in Sunlight); people resist these with \roll{Wits}{Vigilance}.
  \end{description}

  \item[Witnessing Light]
  provides the ultimate test of \pgls{witch} --- only the craftiest find an opportunity to use this \gls{spell}.

  \begin{description}
    \item[Detailed Witness Light]
    \glspl{spell}, provide a clear benefit; they detect any kind of appearance, any face or item at all.
    People can trick these \glspl{spell} easily with false images, which is why so many heroes want their face on a statue --- `for security reasons'.
  \end{description}
\end{description}

\glsadd{Force}
\subsubsection[Force]{\gls{Force}~Force}
manipulates momentum and \glspl{weight}.

\begin{description}
  \item[Waxing Force]
  makes targets heaver, which makes it harder for them to move.
  It also helps increase Damage, but with far more difficulty --- targeting a fast-moving arrow takes skill.

  \item[Waning Force]
  decreases the target's \gls{weight}, making it lighter, or even float.
  In theory, one could make a greatsword very light and easier to wield, but this also reduces the Damage, as you're basically hitting people with a balloon with a sharp edge, so those \glspl{spell} find more use in reducing the \gls{weight} of camping equipment.

  \item[Warping Force]
  turns the flow of heaviness to one side, allowing people to walk across walls instead of across the ground.

  \begin{description}
    \item[Detailed Warp]
    \glspl{spell} rip open gateways in space, allowing \pgls{weight} equal to the \gls{spell}'s targets to pass through.
  \end{description}

  \item[Witnessing Force]
  lets \glspl{witch} detect heavy items, or fast movement.
\end{description}

\glsadd{Life}
\subsubsection[Life]{\gls{Life}~Life}
governs the mess of bones and liquid that make up everything which wanders.
Like Death \glspl{spell}, creatures often resist with their own \glsentrylongpl{hp}, meaning that large, healthy creatures resist these \glspl{spell} easily.

\begin{description}
  \item[Waxing Life]
  makes targets faster, and stronger, but at the cost of an increased metabolism.
  Bigger creatures must feed more, and the super-charged body needs to take in more energy.

  \item[Waning Life]
  means stunting growth or atrophied limbs.
  It directly attacks people's Body \glspl{attribute}.

  \item[Warping Life]
  mutates it, making it grow in strange, new ways.
  These \glspl{spell} add tentacles and wings, claws and legs.

  \item[Witnessing Life]
  means detecting life.
  The standard \gls{tn} is 7 for animals, 10 for \glspl{swarm}, 12 for plants, and 14 for fungi.
\end{description}


\subsection{\Glsfmtplural{descriptor}}
\label{spellDescriptors}
\Glspl{descriptor} increase \pgls{spell}'s properties, giving it a longer range, or copying the \gls{spell} across \pgls{area}.
Each \gls{descriptor} makes the effects more powerful, and makes every other \gls{descriptor} more powerful.

Pour a little more mana into \pgls{spell}, and it blossoms quickly in one direction.
Give a little more to some other part of the \gls{spell}, and both properties swell up even more.

The \gls{spell}-patterns outlined above each cost 1~\gls{mp}.
Characters can spend a number of \glspl{mp} equal to their rating in \pgls{sphere}, so someone with Earth~2 can spend \pgls{mp} to cast a \textit{Wax Air} \gls{spell} (and push someone back with a blast of wind), or spend 2~\glspl{mp} to cast a \textit{Duplicated Wax Air} \gls{spell} (and push many people back, with a more powerful blast of wind).

\glsadd{Detailed}
\subsubsection[Detailed]{\gls{Detailed}~Detailed}
\glspl{casting} let you control the appearance and distinctions of \pgls{spell}.
Where most \glspl{spell} have no more definition than one might expect of a gust of wind or stalagmite, \textit{Detailed} \glspl{spell} look like something.

\begin{boxtable}
  \textbf{Level} & \textbf{Visages}       \\
  \hline
      1          & A lumpy pile of substance. \\
      2          & A candle-flame which seems reminiscent of a face.  A section of river freezing into the shape of a bridge. \\
      3          & \textit{Wane Earth} spells shatter ice, but leave behind impressions of people or beasts which the caster knows well. \\
      4          & Summoned mist looks like the caster's face (or how they think of their own face). \\
      5          & \textit{Warp Fate} spells breed encounters that resemble pivotal moments of the caster's life.  \textit{Wax Death} spread pustules across a target in the shape of a map where the caster lives.  \\
      6          & Every spell looks like the caster, or an intimate face. \\
\end{boxtable}

As the spells gain more detail, they become restricted to the caster's most vivid memories, and eventually cannot represent anything but people or places they love or hate.%
\footnote{Spellcasters who end a close relationship abruptly often haunt themselves for decades through their own spells.
Every storm looks like their hair, every illusion looks all too human, and always like the same human.}
This restricts the possible forms of the spell to people and places the caster knows intimately, and provides a strong clue about the caster's identity, making it difficult to cast spells secretly.

A couple of \glspl{mp} spent on a \textit{Detailed Witness} allow a witch to ascertain if an \textit{oak} fire burns inside a building, or if a casket contains wine.
As before, all questions will only receive a `yes/ no' answer.

\glsadd{Devious}
\subsubsection[Devious]{\gls{Devious}~Devious}
spells are hinted and murmured, but don't require the same flashy gestures as the others, so people often cannot tell when someone casts them.
However, they also take a long time to reach fruition.

\setcounter{track}{1}
\setcounter{spellCost}{1}

The higher the level, the longer the spell will take to reach full power.
They sit dormant for half of the required time, and during the other half, they rise slowly -- one point at a time.

A \textit{Devious Wax Fate} spell which grants $1D6$~\glspl{fp} will wait for half \pgls{interval}, then add half the rolled \glspl{fp}, then the second half thereafter.
A \textit{Devious, Detailed, Wane Fire} spell could wait for two \glspl{interval}, then make a hearth go dim and throw out smoke in the form of a black wolf.
A \textit{Devious, Detailed, Distant, Warp Water} spell cast on a stream could wait for three days, then slowly push an icy replica of the castle which hold the caster prisoner.

If someone notices one of these spells growing, they can put a stop to the spell just like any other.

\glsadd{Distant}
\subsubsection[Distant]{\gls{Distant}~Distant}
magics can strike across vast distances, beyond where even the best archers can reach, and even beyond vision.

However, the caster cannot limit or contain these spells; a \textit{Detailed,  Distant, Wax Air} \gls{spell} begins at `\spellRange',
not `up to a maximum of ``\spellRange'', if you please', but at that minimum and maximum.

The \glspl{spell} have plenty of elasticity within their range, so a spell cast at

`\spellRange'
can reach anywhere \emph{over}

`\spellRange', and \emph{under}

`\spellRange'.

Of course, casters must clearly perceive something in order to cast upon it, so anything outside of clear vision demands a \textit{Distant Witness} precursor.
Once casters can \textit{Witness} someone, they can cast upon them, for the short moment when while the awareness lasts.
Of course, guessing where rivers and houses in the distance might be does not have a high success rate, so setting up these \textit{Distant} spells requires patience, planning, and a way to keep precise records about trajectories.

These \textit{Distant Witness} deductions and maps can take weeks or years of planning for targets far beyond sight.

\begin{exampletext}
  The ability to gain knowledge from a distance allows \glspl{cartographer} and elves to produce excellent maps.
  Combining this \textit{\gls{descriptor}} with \textit{Distant} and \textit{Duplicated}, allows them to inquire about a large area, and then narrow down the space they want to know about.
  Of course, this process requires a lot of skill -- simply locating \pgls{village} from a distance might require listening for something \textit{Detailed} (for the specific type of rock), \textit{Distant}, and \textit{Duplicated}.
  One might then discover the location of a particular person by seeking out the sapphire pendant they wear, using \textit{Detailed Witness Earth}.

  These spells also make long-ranged warfare possible, as casters can send storms, bad luck, or poisoned water after each other.
  Two witches having a duel to the death looks less like a spark-filled inferno of spell-slinging, and more like a protracted game of battleship, with plenty of involvement for bystanders any time one guesses wrong.
\end{exampletext}

\glsadd{Divergent}
\subsubsection[Divergent]{\gls{Divergent}~Divergent}
spells channel mana through opposing \glspl{sphere} to create two parallel, but divergent, effects at the same time.
Check the chart \vpageref{speltogram}, and notice that each \gls{sphere} has two neighbouring \glspl{sphere}, and two opposing \glspl{sphere}.
Divergent spells construct a single spell-sentence to be used by two opposing \glspl{sphere} at the same time.

For example, Water sits between its neighbours Earth and Fate, but opposes Air and Fire.
So a caster with Water 2 and Air 2 could create a \textit{Divergent, Wax Water \& Air} spell, which would make surrounding waters jump and thrash, while wind rages.

These two spells will affect different substances, and the caster can direct them at different targets.

\begin{itemize}
  \item
  A \textit{Divergent Wane Fire, Fate} spell would make \pgls{torch} dim to nothing, while the torch-bearer target loses \glspl{fp}.
  \item
  Encountering some bandits, \pgls{doula} uses a \textit{Duplicated, Divergent, Warp Fire \& Fate} spell.
  Their campfire burns green, and their luck shifts -- the possible forest encounters swing strongly in favour of \glspl{basilisk}.
  \item
  Using \textit{Divergent Witness Water \& Air}, \pgls{pc} could find out if a cavern of air or water existed beneath them (although they would not know which -- only that `yes, one of them exists').
  \item
  While a \textit{Divergent, Duplicated, Wane Mind \& Light} spell would create a large patch of darkness, and plunge anyone inside into confusion.
\end{itemize}

As usual, casters make a single roll to resolve the spell, and apply the result to everything.
A \textit{Divergent, Distant, Wane Earth \& Fate} spell may require a roll of \tn[7] to give someone bad luck, and \tn[14] to make the rock-face above them crumble.
If the caster rolls a total of 13, they would inflict the bad luck, but not make the rock-face crumble.

\glsadd{Duplicated}
\subsubsection[Duplicated]{\gls{Duplicated}~Duplicated}
\label{duplicatedDesc}
spells fork like lightning, affecting every available target nearby.
The caster selects where the spell begins, but cannot decide on the remaining targets, nor can they limit the number.%
\footnote{Each number is simply $n^n$.  So spending 3~\glsfmtplural{mp} means $3^3 = 3\times 3\times 3 = 27$ targets.}

\sidebox[17]{
  \begin{boxtable}[cX]
    \textbf{Level} & \textbf{Targets} \\
    \hline
           1       & 1      \\
           2       & 4      \\
           3       & 27     \\
           4       & 256    \\
           5       & 3,125  \\
           6       & 46,656 \\
  \end{boxtable}
}

When \textit{Duplicated} spells seek out discrete targets (like Fate spells increasing people's \glspl{fp}, or a Fire spell suffocating many candles) each copy of the spell can jump the same range as the original spell's range.
This can present problems; if a caster makes three enemy \glspl{torch} explode in their faces, but the spell requires four targets, then the spell may return to unleash itself on the caster's \gls{torch}.
Spells which cannot find an appropriate target within range just fizzle and die, without effect.

When \textit{Duplicated} spells seek out contiguous targets, they simply affect a wider area
(Water spells can target large sections of a loch, and Earth spells might collapse a cavern).%
\footnote{The incredible range and power of high-level spells means that any attempt at wide-scale wars runs the risk of a skilled caster targeting one, or both, armies with a crippling spell.
  This simple fact has stopped a number of wars before they began.

  However, someone who can fight on, despite blinding fog, a snowstorm, or their \glspl{torch} and camp-fire suddenly going out, will not be more troubled by a spell simply because it affects a thousand more people.
  As a result, a few specialist trackers, or veteran \glspl{guard}, can often fare better against powerful spell-casters than an army of field-workers with swords.}

Casters can often predict whether \pgls{spell} will treat a target as discrete or contiguous, but they have no control over the result.

\subsection{Resolution \& Effects}

\Glspl{spell} may sound very open-ended, or even badly-defined, and they are!
However, the results are not.
The five elemental \glspl{sphere} have a mechanical, numerical result equal to the number of \glspl{mp} spent~+2.

\begin{itemize}
  \item
  A \textit{Wax Earth} spell which solidifies snow over a door, and stops anyone entering, costs 1~\gls{mp}, so it inflicts a -3 penalty to break down the door.
  \item
  A \textit{Duplicated Wane Air} spell which blinds archers with mist requires 2~\glspl{mp}, so it inflicts a -4 penalty to using arrows at any kind of range.
  \item
  A \textit{Divergent, Detailed Wax Fate, \& Fire} spell could bless the caster's ally with a +1 Bonus to using the Melee Skill over their next 5 actions, and make an enemy's \gls{torch} transform into a monstrous face which attempts to eat them.
  The burning \gls{torch} inflicts 5~Damage, which translates to \dmg{5}~Damage.%
  \exRef{core}{Core Rules}{stackingDamage}
\end{itemize}

The final part of the spell is the resistance -- the \gls{gm} stipulates some standard force which stands in the way of the spell.
Sometimes spells have obvious forces acting against them (such as the strength of a rock, when one wants it to crumble), while other spells have less obvious barriers (good-luck spells often have trouble taking effect when the recipients are not paying attention).

\subsubsection{High \Glsfmtplural{sphere}}
\label{highResolution}
work much like the elemental \glspl{sphere}, but the mechanical result equals the \glspl{mp} spent +1 (not `+2', like the elemental \glspl{sphere}).
They have less raw power, but generally target people more directly.
Instead of relying on targets being in water, or under a crumbling cliff-face, \pgls{witch} can combine Water + Earth to use the Life \gls{sphere}, and twist targets' bodies directly.
Where elemental \glspl{sphere} can remove someone's \glspl{fp}, and use blasts of wind to knock them back, a caster with both could combine the effects into the Death \gls{sphere}, and make the targets die.

\subsection{Principles of Magic}

\subsubsection{Bodies in Motion}
run out of `spell-power' if the spell would provide `free fuel', so fire will always burn out without wood, and waves will calm down in time.
The undead burn through the spell which animates them every time they move.
Life spells which bolster a target's Bonuses often demand the target burn through calories in order to keep the spell going, and then fade to nothing once the spell ends.

If you need to track a spell's duration, assume it has a number of points equal to the \glsentrylongpl{mp} used to cast it,%
\footnote{Duplicated spells would divide their initial \glspl{mp} among every copy, but each copy can still rejuvenate itself as if it had been created alone.}
then remove a point every \gls{interval} in which it exerts some force or performs some action.

\subsubsection{Bodies at Rest}
remain unchanged, and the magic remains unspent.
A castle full of deadly Light and Force \glspl{spell} will remain deadly until the \glspl{spell} kill someone; until then, they wait.

Life spells which make tentacles grow out of someone's back will require food to keep the tentacles moving, but if the target simply grows horns, then the horns require no maintenance (although their \gls{weight} will still create a burden).

\subsubsection{Stacking \Glsfmtplural{spell}}
never works with the same kind of spell effect.
Using two Light \glspl{spell} to blind someone has no additional effect -- only the highest counts.
However, a Light spell to blind someone \textit{can} stack with an Air \gls{spell} which blurs someone's vision with mist.

The same principle applies to casters who use Life magic to increase their \gls{dr} -- the second-highest Bonus only counts for half.

\subsubsection{\Glsfmttext{bandAct} \Glsfmtplural{casting}}
work exactly like any other \gls{bandAct}.%
\exRef{core}{Core Rules}{banding}
All casters must be able to cast the spell, and they all spend the \glsentrylongpl{mp}.
This does not increase the spell's potency, it only increases the \gls{casting}~Bonus.

\subsubsection{Potent, Yet Barren}
magic cannot create.
It warps, waxes, and wanes.
It gives information.
But nothing in the world comes from nothing, so spellcasters make the world pulse, or transform, but cannot remove or add the sum of all that is the case.
Witches may control fire, but only a fire striker can \textit{make} it.

\subsubsection{Witnessing is Guesswork}
because all of these spells ultimately produce `yes/ no' answers.
However, they can become wide-spread, and detailed.

In general, a non-Detailed Witness spell can only ask `is there a mind?', or `does this \gls{area} have fire?'.
While a Detailed Witness spell can ask `is there any dwarf over there?', at lower levels.
At the highest levels, the caster can ask about extremely specific targets, but only concerning objects or people they know extremely well.

A sixth-tier Witness spell may only say if one's child is feeling curious about breakfast, but not about whether or not they are alive.

\subsubsection{Think before You Cast!}
because sooner or later someone will fail to see a spell's natural consequences.
Someone may cast a spell to make their enemies shrink and become weak, then notice the spell's range is
\setcounter{spellCost}{4}\setLongRange
`\spellRange', which means the spell cannot target those enemies, so it seeks out the nearest targets at `\spellRange'.
Or perhaps a spell intended to kill all seven bandits has twenty-seven targets, and therefore targets `the bandits, all of the \glspl{pc}, every ally, and the \gls{doula}'s pet magpie'.

Magic creates unintended side-effects, but these side-effects don't fall out of a `random magic effect' table.
They come from players (including the \gls{gm}) misusing magic.

So be forewarned: magic is dangerous.

\end{multicols}

\section{\Glsfmttext{ingredient} Workshop}

\begin{multicols}{2}

\noindent
Spellcasters and alchemist can benefit from rare substances with magical power, called `\glspl{ingredient}'
These include \gls{griffin}~wings, auroch hooves, and dragon eggs.
They can cure diseases, boost spells, and even lock a spell into an item, ready to cast at the right prompt.
They just need prepared correctly.

Preparation needs a clean space, and a clear mind, but never a second set of hands to manage.
Characters cannot use \glspl{restingaction} or Banding actions.

A good space for preparing \glspl{ingredient} grants a Bonus to any roll.

\begin{boxtable}[Lr]
  \textbf{Area} & \textbf{Bonus} \\
  \hline
  Campfire with a pot & +0       \\
  Clean kitchen with a cookery book and larder       & +1       \\
  Grand workshop with three fires, an assortment of pots, and a well-indexed grimoire & +2       \\
  Castle wing with library, glass-blowing room, and a dozen small crushing devices & +3       \\
\end{boxtable}

\subsubsection{Creating \Glsfmtplural{boon}}
requires reducing an \gls{ingredient} into a powder or liquid, slowly, then sealing it quickly to avoid rot; many use cheese-cloth or a phial.

After \pgls{interval}, the character rolls \roll{Intelligence}{Crafts}, at \tn[10].
Success means that the \gls{boon} can boost anyone's rating in the appropriate elemental \gls{sphere} by 1.
A tie indicates that the \gls{boon} emerges useable, but rank.
It will go rotten within $1D6$ days.

With the help of a Fire \gls{boon}, someone without any magical ability could cast a level 1 Fire spell, or a caster with `Fire 2', and `Earth 3' could cast a level 3 Force spell.

A single container might mix multiple \glspl{boon} together, so one container might infuse the air with Fire and Earth.
Someone could use this to boost their Fire Skill, then their Earth Skill; or they might use it in a single Force spell.

\paragraph{Failure}
to make \pgls{boon} properly means the energy releases instantly, resulting in a spell from that \gls{sphere}.
The type of spell depends on the Failure Margin.

\label{randomSpellFailure}
\begin{nametable}[ccL]{\Gls{ingredient} Failures}
  \textbf{Margin} & \textbf{Action} & \textbf{Example} \\
  \hline
    1             & Warp            &  Nearby fires glow blue. \\
    2             & Wane            &  Water evaporates, luck turns bad. \\
    3             & Wax             &  Hearth's ash turns solid, or air blows all the windows open. \\
\end{nametable}

\subsubsection{\Glsfmttext{alchemy}}
\label{alchemicalSpells}
\index{Spells!Alchemical}
only needs \glspl{ingredient} and knowledge to craft spells; it does not need any magical Skill.
Once the alchemist has a `recipe' for the spell, it works like this:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item
  The alchemist gathers a number of \glspl{ingredient} equal to \pgls{spell}'s \gls{sphere} requirement.

  If a spell requires Fate 3, and Water 3, the alchemists needs 3 Fate \glspl{ingredient} and 3 Water \glspl{ingredient}.
  \item
  The player rolls \roll{Intelligence}{Cultivation}.
  The \gls{tn} starts at 6, and each spell level adds 3.
  Rolling a tie means the spell succeeds, but it casts with a +0 Bonus.
  \item
  The spell takes effect the very moment the recipe has completed.
  \begin{itemize}
    \item
    The spell rolls with a Bonus equal to its level, so level 1 \glspl{spell} have a +1~Bonus, and resisting a level 2 \gls{spell} would require a roll at \tn[9].
    \item
    The alchemist can try to flee a moment before the \gls{spell} is completed, but this increases the \gls{tn} by~1.
    \item
    Players can spend \pgls{storypoint} to automatically pass, using the `Random Fact' story (\autopageref{randomFact}).
  \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Failure}
\label{alchemyFailure}
means the spell's energies recombine in the wrong way, producing some random effect, just like \glspl{boon} (check the failures chart \vpageref{randomSpellFailure}).
However, \gls{alchemy} \glspl{spell} can produce much worse failures when they use more mana.

The \glspl{descriptor} are always selected in this order:

\begin{multicols}{2}
  \begin{enumerate}
  \raggedright
    \item
    Divergent (when~possible)
    \item
    Duplicated
    \item
    Detailed
    \item
    Distant
    \item
    Devious
  \end{enumerate}
\end{multicols}

\subsubsection{Creating \Glsfmtplural{talisman}}
works as above, but adds sentience to the mixture, to give the spell a goal.
All spells translate intention into change -- \pgls{talisman} blossoms that intention into a full intelligence with a \textit{Detailed Wax Mind} spell stuffed into the mixture.

The system works as above, with two more steps:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item
  The alchemist needs two Fate \glspl{ingredient}, and two Water \glspl{ingredient} to encourage the spark of mind into rudimentary sentience.
  \item
  A second roll, at \tn[9] must succeed, or the spell casts instantly.
\end{enumerate}

The mind created within \pgls{talisman} does not work like a regular person.
It has extremely limited value and interests.
It will not usually consider its own survival to be interesting -- it only wants to achieve the one thing its creator pushed into it when creating it.
If someone somehow communicates with it, it will not necessarily want to respond, or may only respond by talking about its special interest.
They do not understand deals, and if they did, would have no reason to trust them.

\end{multicols}
